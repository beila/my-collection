2026-11-15 · comment — I've been a frequent critic of starship. But the significance of this milestone is clear: it's the first demonstration of a reusable upper stage of a rocket system since the space shuttle.

Part of the importance is due to the fact that while the space shuttle was a technical success in terms of being able to build and operate a reusable upper stage, it was hella expensive. It required a lot of expensive refurbishment between missions. Launch cadence had a fairly low upper bound. And it wasn't safe enough.

The true believers will claim that catching a starship upper stage marks a new era. It marks the possibility of a new era. It proves it is plausible that a vertical landing upper stage could possibly be a better approach than a space plane. But it hasn't proven any of that. There are about a dozen major milestones between here and actually having a rocket system that meets all of its claimed specifications, both technical and financial. Here's just one big hairy example: to fly manned missions, starship either needs to be redesigned to accommodate an escape system, or it needs to show a level of reliability and safety never previously achieved by a rocket.

In my estimation, it will take at least another five years to know what starship really can do and what it will cost.

2026-11-14 · comment — Starship is, essentially, Space Shuttle done right: fully reusable booster without solid boosters (avoids Challenger disaster) and orbiter stacked on top instead of on the side (avoid Columbia disaster).

The original designs for the Space Shuttle had the booster (with wings!) return to the launch site, but the cost for two new crewed vehicles was too large, so they scaled it back to the compromised version.

Even more revolutionary, compared to Shuttle, Starship is built on an assembly line. They can build a booster ever 4 weeks and a ship every 2 weeks.

The future we were promised in the 1970s is about to become real.

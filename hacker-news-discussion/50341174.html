yurimo · 2026-11-03 · comment — Respectfully to the authors and to everyone here, I ask you to actually read the paper before losing your mind, or at the very least plug it into Claude. It is a cool project but not as nearly as insane as people say. I think the blog writing and subsequent posting overstate the results.

Part of my research is in weight space learning. For starters, we have had the ability to edit weights, to generate new weights, to identify weights for a while. Here is an example for larger models as early as 2023: rome.baulab.info

Weight space interpretability and learning is an active research community, has been for the past 3 years with many great papers. I suspect it is hitting non-interp folks harder because the authors hyped up their work, and people who are not in the field did not bother to read it before typing up X posts.

Some experiments here are done on very small models (20K params). We already know that small models are more interpretable. In fact, much of editing here you can do by hand. For Qwen, the model fixed a looping bug that may have been known apriori, and it is basically a steering vector, a very established technique. This is clear when they then attempt to "improve" math capability, and it doesn't work. For me, this paper rather shows that OpenAI has done training on mechinterp environment, in fact, maybe even on weight space learning datasets, which are open. But it does not show some insane "well past what we’d expect from humans or conventional algorithms" (from the blog post), more like past what I would expect from a large model with no specialized harness. I still think it is cool work, but do check out recent advances in autointerp, to ground your expectation.

On a personal note, I understand the need for promotion of your work, but I think healthy skepticism is fine too. Otherwise, your understanding of what problems need to be worked on and solved in science becomes warped.

yurimo · 2026-11-03 · comment — Scale is an interesting point here. Large overparametrized networks don't behave in the same way as small toy models do; this is one of the big caveats of many interp works. I expect naked weight edit will not work for large models. Why? Because an agent needs to know what it edits and reason on it. You could theoretically improve a model on some form of weight recognition; maybe it will work, maybe it won't. But again, we come up against a cost/benefit dilemma; it sounds exotic and sci-fi but likely impractical. Parameters of the model are connected and relate to each other. Instead, current SOTA auto-interp argues that what we need are better tools for models to know themselves, so rather than direct edit, we need to give models better understanding of large parameter geometries, then they apply edits with non-destructive approaches at scale. This is much harder to do than applying a steering vector.

yurimo · 2026-11-03 · comment — It does, one of my ongoing research papers is about it. But I would ask people to take a look at the established idea of Hypernetworks: arxiv.org/pdf/2306.06955v3 Modern version of the field comes from Google Brain around 2016 arxiv.org/pdf/1609.09106.

In general my personal research thesis is that current deep learning is much less malleable and neuroplastic as it should be, I got into interpretability because of this. Many focus on safety, but I prioritize it as a toolbox that allows for greater shaping of the underlying intelligence system. If we can fully understand how learning happens, where information is stored and how it is processed, unlike in biological brains, we can apply edits and transformations in digital ones. And if I can use a technique to edit weights based on some underlying reasoning about a system (with a caveat that it leads to something worthwhile), so can an agent. Hence self-improvement. There are many interesting implication to weight space research, and I very much encourage people to read about it. But it is also very easy to overstate what is currently possible, in fact I'd say better to know what is not so one can make it possible.

Aurornis · 2026-11-02 · comment — LLMs have been used to investigate and improve LLMs for a long time. Drawing parallels to human brains and neurons isn't all that interesting other than as a philosophical abstraction.

The work done in this experiment could have also been done by a sufficiently motivated human with enough time to explore, though obviously we would need more tools to visualize and track things. There have been a lot of interesting LLM introspection tools that allow you to peek into the model and see what's being activated and run experiments to trigger it.

Are you familiar with abliterated or "uncensored" models where weights have been modified to avoid triggering refusal patterns? You take an LLM, create test cases that trigger the refusal pattern, observe weights involved in that response, then tweak those weights until the response is reduced. This is the same type of work, applied to a specific type of error that was impeding math within Qwen. That work can also be done with an LLM in a harness running the experiments. The amazing part here is that Astra was large enough to walk through the problem without other tooling doing it experimentally.

So I don't think your analogy of a human modifying a neuron is quite applicable. LLM weights are not neurons and they've been inspectable, by humans or LLMs in a harness, for a long time.

A LLM should be considered an advisor to the same extent as legal, financial, and medical. They should be legally required to act to the best of their capability in the interests of their client, subject to the same requirements that they don't break the law to do so as applies to legal, financial, or medical advisors.

An important part of that is that they must never represent themselves as acting for someone who is not their client.

This still allows them to work for you while being the client of their parent companies, but it would disallow claiming to be serving a client when that person is not their client.

When an AI says it is serving your best interests, it must actually be doing so.

The nature of client confidentiality is that it exists because in order to advise appropriately, a full understanding of the client's situation might be necessary. The legal protection of confidentiality enables disclosing the required information without fear of making it public. That too should be protected with AI in your service.

All of this is established principle. I don't see any reason why this should not be applied to AI as well. The same principles have the same reasoning as the current legally protected advisors.

This is an absolute must for the common benefit to society. The alternative is a world where people increasingly trust the ever-more-reasonable sounding AIs while the answer to the question "Who should I vote for?" is available to the highest bidder.

As I've pointed out before, there's extensive legal history in this area. It's called the law of agency. The classic text is Tiffany on Agency.[1] The definition of an agent is: "a person authorized by another, called the principal, to act on his behalf, and represent him, in bringing him into legal relation with a third person." Employees who deal with the outside world are agents. Classic issues for agency are:

- What are the duties of an agent to their principal?

- When an outside party deals with an agent, to what extent can the agent bind the principal?

- When an outside party deals with an agent, what obligations does the agent take on?

Some of these have already been litigated for AI agents. There's the case where an AI agent run by an airline gave a customer advice on fares that was more favorable to the customer than the airline wanted. A judge held that the airline was bound by the actions of its AI agent, just as it would have been if a human had given the same advice. The general rule is that an agent can't bind the principal beyond the limits of their authority as set by the principal. But there are exceptions. There's a reasonableness test. An insane discount might not be upheld by a court. But an employee doing something reasonable which violates a company's instructions might be upheld.

AI agents introduce the problem of outsourced agents. User A asks AI agent B, run by outsourced service C, to do something. This request causes B to buy something from service D. User A didn't want the buy and won't pay. Who's responsible to whom? Can D sue C? D has no preexisting contractual relationship with C which would protect them against D. C may be able in turn to sue A, but that's effort for C. Can D sue A? That's not clear; did A deal with D, or did C deal with D? Contract law is not transitive as regards obligations. This is where those indemnification clauses you skip over in click-through agreements bite.

Much litigation ahead.

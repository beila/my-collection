2026-11-15 · comment — In the United States the Copyright Office actually has guidance on this that I feel most people aren’t aware of.
www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intell...

An excerpt, with emphasis mine:

• Questions of copyrightability and AI can be resolved pursuant to existing law, without the need for legislative change.

• The use of AI tools to assist rather than stand in for human creativity does not affect the availability of copyright protection for the output.

• Copyright protects the original expression in a work created by a human author, even if the work also includes AI-generated material.

• Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is insufficient human control over the expressive elements.

• Whether human contributions to AI-generated outputs are sufficient to constitute authorship must be analyzed on a case-by-case basis.

• Based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not alone provide sufficient control.

• Human authors are entitled to copyright in their works of authorship that are perceptible in AI-generated outputs, as well as the creative selection, coordination, or arrangement of material in the outputs, or creative modifications of the outputs.

• The case has not been made for additional copyright or sui generis protection for AI- generated content.

I am sure there will be many more legal battles and probably legislation about this to come.

2026-11-15 · comment — I think we need to go one level up in the law, and recognize that existing models of copyright and ownership simply don’t apply here, and come up with something new.

There is an undeniable core mathematical principle that should be recognized in law somehow: the emergent capabilities of LLMs are not possible without, effectively, the entire recorded intellectual output of human civilization since the dawn of recorded history as training data.

Trying to determine who “owns” that data, or each individual piece of it, leads us to insane distortions like LLM labs scanning and destroying books. Nobody, including the labs, thinks this is the best solution, but they’re doing it to comply with today’s interpretation of the law.

The reality is that if we are considering the entire corpus of training data, we all own it. We’re not really talking about who owns one book. We’re talking about who can be said to own all the knowledge in all the books. Who can be said to own the existence of books, or the conditions of civilization that cause us to value and preserve books in general.

The preservation and archiving of knowledge that enabled the emergence of LLMs is the product of a system of social relations that belong to the entire civilization. It is a commons. And when we talk about the future impact of these systems, we’re generally talking about taking the value of that commons, and concentrating the value of it in the hands of about a dozen people.

To me, there is no other way to think about a situation like that, where a tiny handful of people realize all the surplus value latent in the entire intellectual inheritance of human civilization, as anything other than theft.

One-off settlements with individual rights holders don't fix that. Neither does scrambling the output of LLMs so that they don’t reproduce their training data verbatim. I don’t think making LLM output non-copyrightable, or all LLM output open source, fixes it either.

I think we need to establish an entirely new framework for thinking about this stuff, and then go forward from there.

2026-11-15 · comment — This article is mostly ragebait, and it's claim is hollow when looked thru the lens of computing history.

Let us skip the whole Bell Labs C/C++/unix/sh/ksh -> BSD csh/vi/bsd/tcp/ip, and other proprietary unix variants/utils and focus instead on today's landscape.

There are two axes we can partition the whole ecosystem. Free/OpenBSD, Clang/LLVM, musl, busybox, X11/Xlib, sqlite, postgresql, nginx, Python, Go, Rust, many embedded rtos, vendor sdk, utilities etc.. and then there is Linux/GCC/glibc, etc. Along the licensing spectrum there is GPL and then the rest of the permissive licenses MIT/BSD/Apache etc where source code publishing is not a requirement.

These permissive license offerings are more prevalent compared to the GPL offerings and as such one can gain all the knowledge needed to build a fully functional system. LLM could memorize, or copy the pattern, so even though it is not a clean room, courts seem to be ok as long as things are not spit out verbatim.

Add to this, the fact the there are tons of research articles, user manuals, RFCs, so one can learn and understand how to build the needed reusable component if small and give their own prompt (and bugs) and and let LLM do as user described.

In the US, purely AI-generated code isn’t copyrightable, but it also doesn’t mean you have to publish it. Also, putting an open-source license on code nobody holds a copyright to has no legal effect. So, ethically or otherwise, there is no strong and convincing argument that open sourcing is the right thing to do.

What 'creators' could do is to compile a list of all the frequently used permissive licenses that are applicable to their system and just make it part of their software license attribution.

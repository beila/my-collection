Charles Petzold

I've been following Sarah Fritz's online writings about Clara Schumann for some time, and we even had dinner together several years ago preceding a performance of Clara Schumann's Piano Concerto at Carnegie Hall. But that won't prevent me from praising to the hilt her stunning new biography, "Madame Composer."

It's significant that "composer" is right in the title, because for much of the 20th century, Clara Schumann the composer was downplayed in favor of Clara Schumann the concert pianist (apparently one of the best of the 19th century), and Clara Schumann the husband of Robert (who died in a sanatorium of syphilis and mental illness, leaving her with seven children), and Clara Schumann the (ahem) "muse" and possibly more to Johannes Brahms. (The "did they or didn't they" question still hangs over any discussion of their relationship.)

"Madam Composer" covers all these aspects of Clara Schumann's remarkable life, but the primary intent is to rescue her marvelous compositions from neglect and sexist disparagement. Throughout "Madam Composer" are quotations from too many men on how they viewed the prospect of women putting musical notes to paper.

If that means that "Madam Composer" is written from an unapologetic feminist perspective, so be it. That is one of its many strengths, because a feminist biography of Clara Schumann is really all about her music.

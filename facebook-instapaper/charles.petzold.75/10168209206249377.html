Towards the end of the first act of Missy Mazzoli's moving opera of "Lincoln in the Bardo," the grieving President has an aria that begins with his memory of his beloved son Willie being measured for his first suit. That suit is now on Willie's dead body as he lies in his crypt. Lincoln has been visiting the crypt at night, opening it up, and holding Willie's body in his arms.

As the aria progresses, Lincoln connects his son's death with the many deaths of both Union and Confederate soldiers, some as young as 16 years old, and we see them slowly moving into the cemetery, compounding Lincoln's grief and guilt. It is 1862, and we know that the War still has three more years to go.

But before the opera is over, Lincoln will have impressed upon him yet another source of pain and grief. On the other side of the fence in the cemetery in which Willie is interred, a mass grave holds the bodies of the enslaved. Although this fact is not mentioned in the opera (or in George Saunders' novel), less than a year after Willie's death, Lincoln will issue the Emancipation Proclamation.

American opera has never been more vibrant and more dead serious.

In the curtain-call photo from last night's performance, from left, is the 12-year-old Everett Sobers, who does an extraordinary job singing the part of Willie, composer Missy Mazzoli, conductor Yannick Nézet-Séguin, librettist Royce Vavrek, baritone Peter Mattei (waving) as Lincoln, and the great soprano Christine Goerke, who appears as the Reverend. (This is one of two cross-dressing roles in the opera; countertenor Anthony Roth Costanzo sings Miss Elise Traynor.)
